01 / THE BASICS

Three doors attackers use

Phishing isn't one technique — it's a family of tricks that all lead to the same place: you handing over something you shouldn't.

Email Phishing

A message posing as your bank, your IT team, or a coworker — built to make you click a link or open a file before you think twice.

Fake Websites

A near-perfect clone of a login page, waiting for you to type in your real password so it can steal it.

Social Engineering

No malware at all — just a phone call, a badge, or a confident tone convincing you to break a rule you'd normally follow.

Flag details

Click a highlighted phrase in the email to reveal what's wrong with it.
01 · Spoofed sender domain
"secure-mail-verify.com" is not your company's real domain. Attackers register lookalike domains that pass a glance but fail on close inspection.
02 · Manufactured urgency
Fear and time pressure are the two most common levers in phishing. Legitimate IT teams rarely threaten suspension within hours.
03 · Generic greeting
"Dear Valued User" instead of your real name suggests a mass campaign, not a message your actual bank or IT team would send.
04 · A threat, not a fact
Specific, dramatic consequences ("locked in 24 hours") are designed to override careful thinking with panic.
05 · Mismatched link
Hovering over a button like this (without clicking) shows the real destination URL — almost never what the button text claims.
06 · Disguised file type
".pdf.exe" is an executable program disguised as a document. Windows often hides the real extension by default — a classic malware trick.

03 / VERIFY

Spotting a fake website

Cloned login pages can look pixel-perfect. The tell is almost always in the address bar, not the page design.

Fake
https://paypa1-secure-login.com/signin
  • Number "1" swapped in for the letter "l"
  • Extra words ("secure-login") bolted onto the real brand name
  • No padlock, or a padlock with a generic certificate
Real
https://www.paypal.com/signin
  • Exact, unmodified brand domain
  • Nothing appended before or after the brand name
  • Valid padlock, certificate matches the domain
→

Hover before you click. The real destination shows in your browser's status bar, even if the link text says something else.

→

Read the domain right to left. The part directly before ".com" is the real owner — everything before that can be faked.

→

Type it yourself. For banking or sensitive logins, type the address directly instead of clicking an emailed link.

→

Check for pressure cues. Countdown timers, "limited slots," and pop-ups demanding immediate login are common fake-site tactics.

04 / HUMAN ANGLE

Social engineering tactics

No link, no attachment — just psychology. These are the four levers used most often.

TACTIC 01

Pretexting

Attacker invents a believable scenario — "I'm from IT, we're migrating your account" — to earn trust before asking for anything.

TACTIC 02

Authority

Impersonating a CEO, auditor, or officer. People hesitate to question someone who sounds senior or official.

TACTIC 03

Urgency & fear

"Do this now or lose access" short-circuits careful thinking and pushes people toward fast, unverified action.

TACTIC 04

Baiting

A tempting offer — a free gift card, a "found" USB drive — that relies on curiosity or greed to get someone to act.

05 / DEFEND

Best practices that actually work

Simple habits, applied consistently, stop the vast majority of phishing attempts.

[ 01 ]

Verify through a second channel

Got an urgent request by email? Confirm it by phone or in person before acting.

[ 02 ]

Enable multi-factor authentication

Even if a password is stolen, MFA stops most account takeovers cold.

[ 03 ]

Never rush a "click now"

Urgency is a manufactured signal. Slowing down for 30 seconds defeats most phishing.

[ 04 ]

Check the sender, not the name

Display names are trivial to fake — always check the actual email address behind it.

[ 05 ]

Report, don't just delete

Reporting a phishing attempt helps your IT/security team block it for everyone else too.

[ 06 ]

Keep software updated

Patches close the exact vulnerabilities phishing payloads are built to exploit.

06 / EVIDENCE

This isn't theoretical

Three well-documented incidents where phishing and social engineering caused real damage.

2013–2015

The fake-vendor invoice scheme

A man ran a long con against two major US tech companies by impersonating a hardware supplier they already worked with, sending convincing fake invoices over email that finance teams paid without extra verification.

~$100M lost combined
2020

The verified-account takeover

Attackers phoned employees at a major social platform, posing as internal IT staff, and social-engineered their way into internal admin tools — no malware involved, just a convincing phone call and a fake login page.

High-profile accounts hijacked in minutes
2011

The "2013 Recruitment Plan" spreadsheet

A well-known security firm was breached after employees opened an Excel attachment from a phishing email with an innocuous-sounding subject line, letting attackers quietly install malware and pivot deeper into the network.

Company's core security product compromised
